## QOL-021 — Spend money to buy back unwanted time when worthwhile


### Idea

Treat time as a resource that can sometimes be purchased, especially when money can remove recurring tasks a person dislikes.

### Why it may affect quality of life

Time pressure can reduce well-being. A purchase that removes unwanted time can change both the activity itself and the subjective feeling of being rushed.

### Evidence

Whillans and colleagues combined cross-national observational studies with a randomized crossover experiment. In the experiment, a time-saving purchase produced less reported time pressure and negative affect than an equally priced material purchase.

### Practical applications

Practical inference: cleaning services, prepared meals, delivery, administrative assistance, or faster transport may apply the principle when they genuinely remove unwanted work.

### Limitations and caveats

The experiment tested a category of time-saving purchase, not every concrete service. The intervention can also fail if money spent creates financial stress or if the saved time is not actually valued.

## QOL-022 through QOL-028 — Concrete ways to buy time


### Idea

Cleaning, grocery delivery, laundry services, meal services, maintenance outsourcing, selective faster transport, and automation can reduce recurring chores.

### Evidence

The upstream evidence supports buying time as a broader class of spending. It does not establish that any one of these services independently improves well-being.

### Practical applications

Practical inference: estimate hours removed, cost per unwanted hour removed, recurring frequency, and whether the saved time is likely to become rest, social connection, exercise, or another valued activity.

### Limitations and caveats

These interventions depend strongly on finances, service quality, preferences, household context, and whether a task is actually unwanted.

## QOL-029 — Use hybrid work when role and circumstances support it


### Idea

Hybrid work can improve satisfaction and retention for some roles without necessarily reducing performance.

### Evidence

A six-month randomized trial of 1,612 Trip.com employees assigned eligible workers to two home-working days per week. Hybrid work improved job satisfaction and retention, with no detected deterioration in performance or promotion outcomes.

### Practical applications

Use hybrid work when the job can support it, considering coordination, home environment, role demands, career stage, and individual preference.

### Limitations and caveats

This was one company and occupational context. It does not establish the same effect for all jobs or all remote-work schedules.

## QOL-030 / QOL-031 — Make recurring travel costs visible


### Idea

A small daily commute cost can become a large annual time cost. Quantifying it makes housing, work, and transport trade-offs easier to evaluate.

### Evidence

The cited sources establish that time pressure can matter and that hybrid work can change satisfaction and retention. They do not directly prove that moving closer to work improves QoL for every commuter.

### Practical applications

Practical inference: calculate minutes per day multiplied by actual travel days per year before comparing housing cost, remote-work options, and alternative transport.

### Limitations and caveats

Housing, social ties, neighborhood quality, job stability, and money may outweigh commute time.

## QOL-080 / QOL-117 — Measure long working hours, then reduce sustained exposure when feasible


### Idea

Measure actual work exposure before deciding whether workload is excessive. When work weeks repeatedly reach 55 hours or more and the work design can be changed, reducing that sustained exposure is a reasonable risk-reduction target.

### Evidence

WHO/ILO population-level evidence identifies working 55 or more hours per week as associated with higher ischemic heart disease and stroke risk compared with 35-40 hours per week.

### Practical applications

Track actual work hours for several representative weeks, including work performed outside formal hours. If prolonged very-long-hour exposure is persistent, address workload, staffing, deadlines, boundaries, or schedule design where possible.

### Limitations and caveats

The 55-hour figure is a population-level exposure category, not a sharp individual biological threshold. The evidence is not a randomized trial showing that one specific reduction in weekly hours produces a guaranteed individual benefit.

- [REF-042](../references/REF-042.md)

## QOL-081 / QOL-082 — Separate being off work from recovering from work


### Idea

Non-work time provides more recovery when attention can disengage from work demands.

### Evidence

Randomized evidence shows cognitive-behavioral and mindfulness strategies can improve psychological detachment. A small pilot recovery-training trial reported broader benefits but needs replication.

### Practical applications

End-of-work rituals, task capture, explicit availability rules, and structured psychological techniques can support detachment when the main problem is mental carryover.

### Limitations and caveats

Individual recovery strategies cannot correct chronic understaffing, unsafe conditions, job insecurity, or other structural work problems.

- [REF-044](../references/REF-044.md)

## QOL-087 / QOL-088 — Financial friction matters when it is actually stressful


### Idea

Financial safety and administrative simplicity can matter when money uncertainty or recurring obligations consume attention.

### Evidence

Longitudinal panel evidence links financial hardship and cash-flow problems with greater risk of mental-health problems. It does not identify one universally sufficient savings target.

### Practical applications

Practical inference: build an emergency buffer suited to circumstances and automate predictable obligations when automation reduces missed tasks or repeated cognitive load.

### Limitations and caveats

Financial advice is context-dependent. Increasing savings at the expense of basic needs or valued life activities is not automatically a QoL improvement.

- [REF-012](../references/REF-012.md)

## QOL-100 — Audit recurring commitments


### Idea

Recurring commitments deserve periodic review because their time cost repeats indefinitely.

### Practical applications

Practical inference: ask whether a recurring obligation would still be chosen today, then compare its current value with its time, money, and recovery cost.

### Limitations and caveats

Some responsibilities cannot or should not be removed merely because they are unpleasant. The point is to identify optional commitments whose original value has disappeared.
