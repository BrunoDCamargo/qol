## QOL-052 — Reduce reliance on ultraprocessed foods


### Idea

Build a dietary pattern around minimally processed, nutrient-dense foods rather than relying heavily on ultraprocessed products.

### Why it may affect quality of life

Food processing can change energy density, eating rate, palatability, and how much people consume even when presented diets are matched on several nutritional variables.

### Evidence

In a controlled inpatient randomized crossover trial, 20 adults consumed about 500 kcal/day more and gained weight during an ultraprocessed diet period compared with a minimally processed diet period. Current U.S. dietary guidance also emphasizes nutrient-dense foods and reducing highly processed foods.

### Practical applications

Favor meals built around vegetables, fruits, legumes, whole or fiber-rich grains, and other minimally processed foods that fit the person's dietary needs.

### Limitations and caveats

The inpatient RCT was small and short. Food processing is not the only determinant of weight or health, and individual foods should not be classified only by a moralized healthy/unhealthy binary.

## QOL-053 — Design the food environment so preferred choices are easy defaults


### Idea

Make the foods someone intends to eat easy to access and prepare, reducing the repeated decision cost of choosing them.

### Why it may affect quality of life

Eating behavior occurs in an environment. Availability, preparation effort, portioning, and convenience can shape repeated choices.

### Evidence

The cited sources support dietary patterns and show that food form can affect intake. They do not directly test a general household rule of making preferred foods the easiest option.

### Practical applications

Practical inference: keep suitable foods available, pre-portion frequently overeaten foods, prepare components in advance, or use services that make the intended pattern easier to sustain.

### Limitations and caveats

This is a behavioral-design inference. It should not be presented as a clinical treatment for eating disorders or as proof that one food-environment strategy works for everyone.

## QOL-054 / QOL-055 — Use adequate hydration, not maximal hydration


### Idea

Use water and unsweetened drinks as routine hydration choices and correct inadequate fluid intake when it exists.

### Why it may affect quality of life

Dehydration can affect normal physiological function and may contribute to symptoms such as unclear thinking, overheating, or constipation.

### Evidence

CDC guidance notes that water prevents dehydration and that needs vary by age, activity, climate, illness, and other factors. Current U.S. dietary guidance recommends water and unsweetened beverages.

### Practical applications

Choose water as a default beverage and increase intake when activity, heat, illness, or another context raises fluid needs.

### Limitations and caveats

More water is not automatically better. Some medical conditions require individualized fluid guidance.

## QOL-004 / QOL-056 — Treat caffeine dose and timing as variables


### Idea

If sleep may be affected by caffeine, test timing and dose before assuming complete abstinence is necessary.

### Evidence

A controlled study found that 400 mg of caffeine disrupted sleep even when taken 6 hours before bedtime.

### Practical applications

Move the last caffeine dose earlier or reduce dose and observe sleep over a meaningful period.

### Limitations and caveats

The study tested a relatively high 400 mg dose. Individual caffeine metabolism and sensitivity vary, so its exact six-hour result cannot be applied mechanically to every dose or person.

## QOL-057 / QOL-058 — Substance reductions are conditional high-value interventions


### Idea

Alcohol and tobacco/nicotine are only actionable QoL targets when there is meaningful exposure.

### Evidence

CDC and WHO describe substantial health risks from alcohol and tobacco exposure. Tobacco cessation has a particularly strong preventive-health case.

### Practical applications

Reduce alcohol when it creates health, sleep, mood, or behavioral problems. Stop tobacco use when applicable, using evidence-based cessation support as needed.

### Limitations and caveats

People with physiological dependence may need clinical support. Abrupt alcohol cessation can be dangerous in severe dependence.

## QOL-066 / QOL-067 / QOL-068 — Supplements should answer a defined question


### Idea

Avoid treating supplements or broad deficiency screening as generic optimization. Tie testing or supplementation to a defined indication, deficiency risk, or evidence-based use.

### Evidence

USPSTF recommendations do not support assuming broad preventive benefit from generic multivitamin use and find insufficient evidence for population vitamin-D deficiency screening in asymptomatic adults. NCCIH emphasizes variable evidence, safety, and interaction risks across supplements.

### Practical applications

Use a supplement because a specific nutritional, clinical, or evidence-based need exists, and review interactions when medicines are also used.

### Limitations and caveats

These recommendations do not argue against treating a documented deficiency or using a supplement with a specific supported indication.

## QOL-101 through QOL-106 — Treat diet quality as more than food processing


### Idea

Diet quality includes food sources, fibre, free sugars, sodium, and fat type in addition to degree of processing.

### Evidence

WHO guidelines support whole grains, vegetables, fruits, and pulses as primary carbohydrate sources, at least 25 g/day of naturally occurring fibre for adults, lower free-sugar and sodium intake, and replacement of saturated/trans fats with unsaturated fats or naturally fibre-rich carbohydrate sources. WHO also conditionally recommends lower-sodium potassium-containing salt substitutes for appropriate adults who use discretionary salt.

### Limitations and caveats

The salt-substitute recommendation is not universal. Kidney impairment and other conditions affecting potassium excretion can make potassium-containing substitutes inappropriate, and the WHO recommendation does not apply universally to pregnancy or children.

## QOL-107 — Use structured obesity treatment when indicated


### Idea

When obesity is clinically relevant, use a structured multicomponent intervention rather than relying on a single isolated diet or exercise instruction.

### Evidence

USPSTF recommends offering or referring adults with obesity to intensive, multicomponent behavioral interventions.

### Limitations and caveats

This item does not define one universal target weight or exclude medications, surgery, or other evidence-based treatment when indicated.
