## QOL-012 — Trial reduced or blocked mobile internet on smartphones

**Categories:** attention, technology, mental-health  
**Evidence:** Moderate  
**Applicability:** General

### Idea

Reduce the phone's ability to provide continuous internet access while preserving other communication functions when feasible.

### Why it may affect quality of life

A smartphone with continuous internet creates a high-frequency opportunity for checking, context switching, and passive consumption throughout the day.

### Evidence

A randomized study found that a two-week mobile-internet block improved subjective well-being, mental health, and sustained attention on average. Adherence was incomplete, which limits generalization.

### Practical applications

A time-limited experiment can block mobile data or selected internet access while preserving calls and essential communication.

### Limitations and caveats

The trial does not establish that everyone should permanently remove smartphone internet. Participants' motivation and adherence matter.

## QOL-013 / QOL-014 — Reduce externally triggered checking

**Categories:** attention, technology, work-design  
**Evidence:** Moderate for notifications; Inference for interface cues  
**Applicability:** General

### Idea

Reserve interruption rights for genuinely important events instead of letting every application compete for attention.

### Evidence

A field experiment found that disabling communication notifications reduced interruptions and strain and improved performance during the studied work period.

### Practical applications

Practical inference: remove badges, banners, sound, and vibration from nonessential applications because these cues serve the same interruption pathway, although each interface element was not independently randomized in the cited experiment.

### Limitations and caveats

Some roles require rapid response. Notification reduction should preserve safety-critical or genuinely time-sensitive channels.

## QOL-017 / QOL-018 / QOL-032 — Reduce avoidable context switching

**Categories:** attention, work-design, technology  
**Evidence:** Moderate for interruption/multitasking effects; Inference for scheduling tactics  
**Applicability:** Mixed

### Idea

Create periods in which one cognitively demanding task can proceed without unnecessary switching.

### Evidence

A randomized experiment found stress responses under multitasking and interruption conditions. Notification research also demonstrates that externally triggered interruptions alter strain and performance.

### Practical applications

Practical inference: batch asynchronous communication, use focus blocks, or create meeting-free periods when response latency is acceptable.

### Limitations and caveats

Monotasking is not appropriate for every job. The goal is to remove unnecessary switching rather than eliminate legitimate coordination.

## QOL-020 — Treat generic social-media detox claims cautiously

**Categories:** technology, mental-health, attention  
**Evidence:** Moderate  
**Applicability:** General

### Idea

Evaluate the specific behavior and outcome instead of assuming that abstaining from social media will broadly improve every mental-health measure.

### Evidence

In a 14-day randomized experimental study, abstinence reduced screen time and body-image dissatisfaction but did not produce clear between-group improvements in depression, anxiety, FoMO, or loneliness.

### Limitations and caveats

Platforms, baseline use, age, motivations, and interventions differ. This caution does not contradict evidence for reducing continuous smartphone internet access, which is a different intervention.

## QOL-075 / QOL-090 — Manage screen-related fatigue with symptoms in mind

**Categories:** vision, technology, attention, work-design  
**Evidence:** Moderate  
**Applicability:** Conditional

### Idea

When prolonged near-screen work causes eye fatigue or cognitive fatigue, use breaks as a symptom-management strategy rather than waiting for discomfort to become severe.

### Evidence

A controlled crossover experiment found that break schedules reduced some digital-eye-strain symptoms. A meta-analysis found small benefits of microbreaks for fatigue and vigor.

### Practical applications

Use short visual or movement breaks during long work periods and reassess vision if symptoms persist despite reasonable screen practices.

### Limitations and caveats

Breaks do not correct refractive error, ocular disease, or all causes of fatigue.
