## QOL-042 through QOL-045 — Treat the bedroom as an exposure environment

**Categories:** sleep, environment, circadian  
**Evidence:** Mixed  
**Applicability:** Conditional except for daytime light

### Idea

Noise, light, and thermal discomfort are repeated exposures because sleep occupies a large fraction of life. Correct an environmental deficit when it is actually present.

### Evidence

WHO identifies environmental noise as a health exposure. Controlled sleep studies support effects of noise reduction and show that thermal conditions can influence sleep, although the thermal evidence in this registry is small. Daytime light and darkness are part of circadian regulation.

### Practical applications

Practical inference: use source reduction, window treatments, an eye mask, earplugs, ventilation, fans, cooling, heating, or bedding changes based on the observed deficit.

### Limitations and caveats

There is no evidence here for a universally ideal bedroom temperature, sound profile, or darkness intervention. Match the intervention to an actual problem.

## QOL-046 — Use source control/exhaust ventilation while cooking

**Categories:** indoor-air, environment, preventive-health  
**Evidence:** High  
**Applicability:** Conditional

### Idea

When cooking generates particulate pollution, capture or remove emissions at the source rather than relying only on room-wide filtration.

### Evidence

EPA identifies cooking and combustion as indoor particulate sources and recommends source control and exhaust ventilation to the outdoors.

### Practical applications

Use an externally vented range hood when available and appropriate, and maintain equipment so it actually exhausts as designed.

### Limitations and caveats

Indoor-air exposures vary with cooking method, fuel, ventilation, home layout, and outdoor air quality.

## QOL-047 — Use HEPA filtration conditionally

**Categories:** indoor-air, environment, preventive-health  
**Evidence:** High  
**Applicability:** Conditional

### Idea

Use filtration when there is a meaningful particulate exposure or another relevant reason, rather than as a universal wellness purchase.

### Evidence

EPA states that portable air cleaners and HVAC filters can reduce some indoor pollutants, while source control and ventilation remain primary strategies.

### Practical applications

Select a device appropriately sized for the room and pollutant type, and address the source when possible.

### Limitations and caveats

No filter removes all pollutants. Some air-cleaning technologies can themselves generate undesirable byproducts.

## QOL-048 — Do not turn CO2 into a universal cognitive score

**Categories:** indoor-air, environment, attention  
**Evidence:** Moderate  
**Applicability:** General

### Idea

CO2 can be a useful ventilation proxy in some contexts, but a consumer sensor reading should not be interpreted as a direct cognitive-performance meter.

### Evidence

A 2026 randomized controlled exposure study of 398 adults found no systematic cognitive decline across the tested acute CO2 conditions, including high concentrations.

### Limitations and caveats

This does not make ventilation irrelevant. CO2, particles, VOCs, humidity, temperature, and infectious-aerosol risk are different environmental variables.

## QOL-084 — Workplace vegetation is a small, low-stakes intervention

**Categories:** work-design, environment, mental-health  
**Evidence:** Low  
**Applicability:** Conditional

### Idea

Plants may modestly alter perceived restoration or stress in a workspace, but they should remain below larger determinants such as workload, control, noise, and physical activity.

### Evidence

A small crossover experiment found lower reported stress and fatigue after an hour of work in a room with plants compared with a room without plants.

### Limitations and caveats

The study was small and short. Plants are an environmental preference, not a treatment for occupational stress.

## QOL-098 / QOL-099 — Remove repeated physical friction

**Categories:** friction-reduction, environment, time  
**Evidence:** Inference  
**Applicability:** Conditional

### Idea

A small irritation can become meaningful when it repeats daily: a broken fixture, unreliable cable, uncomfortable object, or item that must constantly be moved between locations.

### Evidence

The cited evidence supports the broader value of spending resources to save unwanted time. It does not directly test repairing a specific household object or buying a duplicate charger.

### Practical applications

Practical inference: repair, replace, relocate, or duplicate a low-cost object when doing so reliably removes repeated annoyance, searching, setup, or transport.

### Limitations and caveats

This principle can become an excuse for unnecessary consumption. Require a real recurring friction before applying it.
