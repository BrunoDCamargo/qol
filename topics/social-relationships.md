## QOL-062 / QOL-063 — Social connection is more than event count


### Idea

Assess the structure, function, and quality of relationships rather than reducing social health to the number of social events attended.

### Why it may affect quality of life

Social isolation and loneliness affect health and well-being, while satisfying connection can support belonging, practical help, meaning, and resilience.

### Evidence

The WHO Commission on Social Connection identifies social connection as a multidimensional determinant of health and well-being.

### Practical applications

Practical inference: reliable recurring contact with valued people can make connection less dependent on repeated scheduling decisions and occasional large gatherings.

### Limitations and caveats

Frequency alone does not establish connection quality. Some people need less contact than others, and unwanted social obligations can reduce rather than improve well-being.

## QOL-064 — More social activity is not automatically a loneliness treatment


### Idea

Match loneliness interventions to the mechanism instead of assuming that adding activities solves the subjective experience of disconnection.

### Evidence

A pragmatic cluster-randomized trial of 469 lonely or socially isolated adults tested facilitated connection to community resources and activities. It did not produce clinically meaningful improvement in the primary or secondary outcomes.

### Limitations and caveats

A null result for one intervention does not mean social connection is unimportant or that no social intervention can work. It means activity quantity alone should not be assumed to resolve loneliness.

### References

- [REF-031](../references/REF-031.md)

## QOL-065 — Prosocial acts are not a universal loneliness treatment


### Idea

Helping others can be meaningful, but it should not be prescribed as a guaranteed fix for loneliness.

### Evidence

A randomized prosociality intervention found heterogeneous effects and no consistent average loneliness reduction across participants.

## QOL-078 — Treat persistent relationship distress when it drives QoL


### Idea

When a close relationship is itself a major source of recurring distress, relationship-focused treatment may address the problem more directly than unrelated wellness changes.

### Evidence

Behaviorally based couple therapies have randomized clinical evidence with long-term follow-up showing meaningful improvement for some distressed couples.

### Limitations and caveats

Relationship problems differ in cause and safety context. Couple therapy is not appropriate for every relationship or every form of conflict.

## QOL-079 — Include sexual health when relevant


### Idea

Sexual health belongs in a complete quality-of-life map when pain, function, desire, fertility concerns, identity, safety, or relationship effects matter to the person.

### Evidence

WHO defines sexual health as involving physical, emotional, mental, and social well-being related to sexuality.

### Limitations and caveats

The item is an assessment principle, not a claim that more sexual activity universally improves quality of life.

## QOL-085 / QOL-086 — Spending and relationships depend on context


### Idea

Money can support connection when it enables valued shared experiences, but social spending should not be treated as a formula for happiness.

### Evidence

Experimental work suggests that social involvement helps explain when experiences outperform material purchases in well-being. A close replication of the classic prosocial-spending experiment produced mixed evidence rather than a clean universal effect.

### Limitations and caveats

The value of an experience depends on preferences, companions, financial constraints, and context.
