## QOL-002 — Evaluate possible sleep apnea when signs are present

**Categories:** sleep, preventive-health  
**Evidence:** High  
**Applicability:** Conditional

### Idea

Treat symptoms such as loud snoring, witnessed breathing pauses, gasping, or significant daytime sleepiness as reasons to consider a clinical sleep-apnea evaluation rather than assuming that more time in bed will solve the problem.

### Why it may affect quality of life

Sleep-disordered breathing can fragment sleep and impair daytime function even when total time in bed appears adequate.

### Evidence

NHLBI lists loud snoring, breathing pauses, gasping and daytime symptoms among common sleep-apnea features.

### Practical applications

Clinical evaluation can include history, examination, and sleep testing when indicated.

### Limitations and caveats

Snoring alone does not establish sleep apnea. This item applies when symptoms or risk factors justify assessment.

## QOL-003 — Use CBT-I for chronic insomnia when indicated

**Categories:** sleep, mental-health  
**Evidence:** High  
**Applicability:** Conditional

### Idea

Use structured cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia when chronic insomnia is present instead of relying only on generic sleep-hygiene advice.

### Why it may affect quality of life

Persistent insomnia directly affects sleep continuity, daytime energy, distress, and functioning.

### Evidence

A pragmatic multicentre randomized trial found that group CBT-I reduced insomnia severity and increased clinically relevant improvement versus waiting-list control.

### Practical applications

CBT-I can be delivered in structured individual, group, or validated digital formats depending on access and clinical context.

### Limitations and caveats

CBT-I is a treatment for insomnia, not a universal sleep optimization protocol for people who already sleep well.

## QOL-007 / QOL-008 — Treat sound as an environmental variable

**Categories:** sleep, environment  
**Evidence:** High for harmful environmental noise; Moderate for specific masking strategies  
**Applicability:** Conditional

### Idea

Reduce noise that actually disrupts sleep. Do not assume that adding continuous white or pink noise is automatically beneficial.

### Why it may affect quality of life

Intermittent environmental noise can produce arousals and sleep fragmentation.

### Evidence

WHO treats environmental noise as a health exposure. In a controlled sleep-laboratory study, earplugs mitigated some effects of intermittent environmental noise, while pink noise was not uniformly beneficial and reduced REM sleep.

### Practical applications

Practical inference: source reduction, window sealing, room selection, or earplugs may be reasonable when external noise is a real sleep disruptor.

### Limitations and caveats

The best intervention depends on the source and the sleeper. Masking sound is different from reducing the source.

## QOL-009 / QOL-045 — Keep the bedroom thermally comfortable

**Categories:** sleep, environment  
**Evidence:** Low  
**Applicability:** Conditional

### Idea

Treat heat, humidity, airflow, and thermal comfort as variables when they cause awakenings or discomfort.

### Why it may affect quality of life

Thermal discomfort can interfere with sleep initiation, continuity, and perceived restoration.

### Evidence

A small randomized crossover trial found differences in sleep efficiency, latency, and subjective refreshment under two cooling/dehumidification conditions at the same nominal room temperature.

### Practical applications

Practical inference: ventilation, fans, air conditioning, bedding changes, or dehumidification can be tested when thermal discomfort is observable.

### Limitations and caveats

The direct trial was very small and does not establish a universal ideal bedroom temperature.

- [REF-072](../references.md#ref-072)

## QOL-010 — Use darkness interventions when light is actually disruptive

**Categories:** sleep, environment, circadian  
**Evidence:** Moderate  
**Applicability:** Conditional

### Idea

Reduce unwanted bedroom light when it interferes with sleep.

### Why it may affect quality of life

Darkness is part of circadian signaling, and external light can create a modifiable sleep-environment problem.

### Evidence

A randomized trial in sleep-deprived women in late pregnancy found longer actigraphy-measured night sleep and better self-reported sleep quality with eye-mask plus earplugs than with a sleep-advice leaflet.

### Practical applications

Practical inference: blackout curtains may apply the same light-reduction principle, but the cited trial directly tested an eye mask plus earplugs, not blackout curtains.

### Limitations and caveats

The RCT population was late-pregnancy women with short sleep, so generalization to all sleepers is uncertain.

## QOL-069 — Keep sleep/wake timing reasonably regular

**Categories:** sleep, circadian, preventive-health  
**Evidence:** Moderate  
**Applicability:** General

### Idea

Consider sleep timing and day-to-day regularity alongside sleep duration.

### Why it may affect quality of life

Regular timing supports alignment between behavioral sleep schedules and circadian physiology.

### Evidence

A National Sleep Foundation consensus panel concluded that sleep regularity is an important dimension of healthy sleep, while noting gaps in evidence for exact thresholds.

### Practical applications

Maintain reasonably consistent sleep and wake times when feasible rather than creating large recurring shifts between workdays and free days.

### Limitations and caveats

There is no single schedule that suits everyone, and shift work or caregiving may make regularity difficult.
